# Interview: airline operations controller

Label: role-play, not evidence. Pre-seeded by the facilitator. Method 1b.

## Can see

Fuel, crew duty time, passenger count for the 14 aircraft in this airline's fleet. Divert field agreements. Phone to each captain through dispatch.

## Cannot see

The other 26 aircraft. Radar. Vespyra. The tower frequency.

## Questions and answers

**Which of your aircraft cannot divert?**
Two cannot reach Portland. One is a regional jet that planned fuel to the minute, one is a heavy with a medical case on board that should not spend another forty minutes in the air. Three more can reach Paine or Boeing Field but not Portland. I know this in thirty seconds from my screen. Assume and label: two hard, three soft.

**Who do you tell?**
My captains, through dispatch. Not approach. Approach is not on my phone. If approach asks my captain, my captain answers, but approach does not know to ask the right one first.

**What do the other five airlines know about their fleets?**
The same as me about theirs, I assume. We do not share it. There is no reason to on a normal day. Assume and label.

**What happens if approach sends your heavy to Portland?**
The captain refuses, approach reshuffles, and that costs two minutes of frequency time nobody has. It would not be approach's fault. They cannot see what I see.

**What would you want?**
To push a divert-capability flag per aircraft to whoever is sequencing, once, before they start asking. Not a shared system. A one-way feed.

## Coach observations

- The fuel gap is not an information gap. The information exists, in six places, in thirty seconds each. It is a sharing gap.
- A one-way flag from airline ops to approach may be most of the software. H1 narrows.
- "Approach cannot see what I see" is the fourth interview in a row to describe the same seam from a different side.
